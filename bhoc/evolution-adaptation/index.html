Evolution & biological adaptation

Evolution built a system around hemoglobin.

Hemoglobin is more than a molecule that happens to carry oxygen. Across vertebrate evolution, globin-based oxygen binding became a highly successful molecular solution, and multiple layers of biology developed around it to protect, regulate and deploy that function.

In development

The red blood cell is one of those layers. It packages hemoglobin inside a specialized cellular environment. Blood flow, vascular tone, ventilation, erythropoiesis, microcirculation, tissue diffusion and metabolic demand add further levels of control. The result is not a single oxygen carrier but a multi-level oxygen-delivery architecture.

Different species adapt different parts of that architecture to their physiology and environment. The details vary, but the biological problem remains the same: oxygen must be loaded, transported, released and ultimately made available to living tissue. Understanding those layers helps explain both the power of the natural red-cell system and why BHOC focuses on one defined function within it: oxygen delivery.

Knowledge boundary

This BHOC page is the knowledge map, not a duplicate textbook. It connects what hemoglobin is, how globins evolved, how hemoglobin binds and releases oxygen, how affinity and physiology change function, and how oxygen biology varies across species and human populations.

These concepts must stay separate: hemoglobin variants are not blood groups; blood groups are primarily red-cell surface antigen systems; species-specific transfusion compatibility is another layer; and HBOC/BHOC technologies move the oxygen-carrying function outside the intact red-cell system.

For human biology we use scientifically precise language such as genetic and population variation, hemoglobin variants and blood-group systems rather than treating “race” as a biological category.
