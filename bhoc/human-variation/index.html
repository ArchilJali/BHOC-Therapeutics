Human Variation, Blood Diversity and the Future of Oxygen Transport

A bioengineering perspective on the biological architecture of blood, antigenic diversity and the fundamental function of oxygen transport.

Archil Jaliashvili · BHOC Therapeutics · 25 November 2026

Human blood is among the most extensively studied biological systems. Yet its diversity extends across multiple cellular and molecular layers: red-cell antigens, platelet antigens, leukocyte antigens, plasma proteins and inherited molecular variation. This complexity matters. It also invites a broader question: how should we distinguish the biological identity of blood from the physiological function that the body depends on it to perform?

1. Biological diversity at planetary scale

Life on Earth is extraordinarily diverse. A frequently cited contemporary count is approximately 2.17 million described and named species. This is a count of documented species in a particular compilation, not an estimate of the total number of species that exist or may exist. The distinction is essential: catalogued diversity and total biological diversity are not interchangeable.

Human biology is one part of this larger living system. Within our own species, inherited variation produces differences in proteins, cell-surface structures and immune recognition. Blood is a particularly clear example of how biological variation can accumulate across several interacting systems.

2. Blood diversity is multidimensional

Blood is not defined by a single compatibility label. Red blood cells express antigens organized into numerous blood-group systems. Platelets carry human platelet antigens; leukocytes express HLA and human neutrophil antigens; plasma contains additional proteins and immunologically relevant factors. These systems have different biological roles and are not interchangeable.

Consequently, a simplified label such as ABO/Rh describes only a limited part of the immunohematological landscape. The clinical relevance of any antigen or antibody depends on the component, the patient, the exposure history and the clinical context. This chapter does not propose replacing established compatibility testing or transfusion practice.

3. A theoretical illustration of combinatorial complexity

The accompanying theoretical model combines selected antigenic dimensions to illustrate how rapidly a profile space can expand when multiple independent systems are considered together. Under the model's stated assumptions, the arithmetic yields:

2.59 × 10134

This value is a model-derived combinatorial illustration. It is not a measured count of unique human blood phenotypes, not a probability that every combination occurs, and not a validated estimate of the number of clinically distinct blood profiles.

Interpretation boundary. The calculation assumes independence among the selected dimensions and uses simplified representation counts. Linkage, population frequencies, allele distributions, antigen expression, biological constraints and incomplete coverage of relevant systems are not fully represented. The model therefore illustrates potential combinatorial scale rather than empirical prevalence.

4. One fundamental physiological function

Despite this diversity, one of blood's central physiological functions is oxygen transport. In the lungs, hemoglobin binds oxygen; circulation carries oxygenated blood through the vascular network; oxygen is unloaded into tissues according to local conditions and metabolic demand. Carbon dioxide generated by metabolism is transported back toward the lungs for elimination, through several dissolved and chemically converted forms.

Hemoglobin is central to this process, but oxygen delivery is not determined by hemoglobin concentration alone. It also depends on saturation, blood flow, microvascular distribution, diffusion distance, unloading characteristics and tissue demand. The final biological objective is not simply oxygen carried in a vessel, but oxygen made available where it is needed.

5. From biological diversity to Precision Oxygen Therapeutics

This distinction between biological identity and physiological function provides a useful bioengineering perspective. Blood performs many functions: gas transport, immune defence, hemostasis, buffering, transport of nutrients and metabolites, and more. A hemoglobin-based oxygen carrier is not a substitute for all of these functions.

BHOC — Biological Hemoglobin Oxygen Carrier — is framed within Precision Oxygen Therapeutics as an approach focused on the oxygen-transport function. The scientific question is how hemoglobin-based oxygen transport may support tissue oxygen delivery in defined settings where native oxygen transport is insufficient. This is a function-focused development concept, not a claim that one product replaces whole blood or resolves every dimension of transfusion compatibility.

The broader proposition is therefore straightforward: biological diversity should be understood in its full complexity, while therapeutic design should identify and evaluate a specific physiological function with precision.
