Artificial Blood & Blood Substitutes: What Changed?

The history of hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers is often compressed into one sentence: “blood substitutes failed.” The scientific record is more useful than that. Important trials failed, serious safety signals were identified, and the field learned that moving hemoglobin outside the red blood cell changes its biology. It also learned that different HBOCs are not chemically or physiologically identical. The central question therefore changed from “Can we replace blood?” to “Can we support a defined oxygen-delivery function with a product designed and tested for a specific context?”

01 · Historical assumption

The phrase “blood substitute” set an impossibly broad target.

Blood is not one therapeutic function. It transports oxygen and carbon dioxide, provides hemostatic components, contributes to immune defense, buffering, rheology and volume, and carries cells and proteins with functions far beyond oxygen transport. Yet for decades, “artificial blood” and “blood substitute” became common labels for technologies that often performed only one or two of these functions.

BloodBiological systemMultiple cellular, hemostatic, immune, transport and regulatory functions.
RBCCellular oxygen architectureHemoglobin packaged inside a metabolically and structurally regulated cell.
HBOCDefined oxygen-carrier technologyHemoglobin oxygen-carrying function moved outside the intact red-cell system.

That distinction matters because a technology designed to support oxygen transport should not automatically be judged as though it must reproduce every property of whole blood or an intact red-cell transfusion. At the same time, removing hemoglobin from the red cell creates new physiological constraints that must be addressed rather than ignored.

02 · The pivotal safety signal

Natanson 2008 changed the field — and the warning must remain part of the story.

In 2008, Natanson and colleagues published a JAMA meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials involving five different cell-free hemoglobin-based products and 3,711 patients across surgical, stroke and trauma populations. The pooled analysis reported a statistically significant increase in mortality (RR 1.30; 95% CI 1.05–1.61) and myocardial infarction (RR 2.71; 95% CI 1.67–4.40). The authors concluded that the evaluated hemoglobin-based blood substitutes were associated with increased risks of death and myocardial infarction.

This signal should not be minimized.

It identified a serious safety problem in the clinical development record available at the time and forced the field to confront the biological consequences of cell-free hemoglobin, including vascular and oxidative mechanisms.

The correct scientific response is therefore not to erase Natanson. It is to ask a second question: what exactly was being pooled, which mechanisms were shared, which properties differed, and what did later molecular characterization teach us about those differences?

03 · Product heterogeneity

“HBOC” is a category. It is not one chemical entity.

In the same scientific period, FDA researchers Paul Buehler and Abdu Alayash emphasized that all hemoglobin-based oxygen carriers are not created equally. Chemical modification can change allostery, oxygen affinity, oxidative and nitrosative reactions, heme stability, molecular size, exposure and interaction with endogenous hemoglobin-clearance mechanisms.

A later 2018 side-by-side biochemical and biophysical characterization of clinically evaluated HBOCs reinforced this point. Human-, bovine- and genetically engineered products showed materially different oxygen-equilibrium, ligand-binding, autoxidation, redox and heme-release characteristics. These are not cosmetic differences. They are variables capable of changing biological behavior.

The scientific correction is not “the trials did not matter.”

The correction is that a class-level clinical signal and a product-level development decision are different questions. Shared mechanisms can exist across a class while individual products still differ substantially in potency, kinetics, vascular behavior, oxidative stability and benefit–risk for a defined indication.

04 · What science learned

Moving hemoglobin outside the RBC changes the architecture around the molecule.

The modern lesson is mechanistic. Free or modified extracellular hemoglobin interacts with the vascular system differently from hemoglobin compartmentalized inside an erythrocyte. Important variables include nitric-oxide consumption, molecular size and extravasation, P50 and oxygen unloading, solution rheology, autoxidation, methemoglobin formation, heme release, oxidative reactions, exposure time and the physiological state in which the product is used.

Recent reviews continue to distinguish HBOCs by their biochemical and biophysical characteristics and explicitly argue that each product and indication requires its own assessment. A 2025 commentary in Transfusion states that efficacy depends on product characteristics, preclinical and clinical findings, and the indication proposed, and notes that benefit–risk can differ substantially with clinical context.

05 · New understanding

The question becomes narrower — and more useful.

BHOC does not use Artificial Blood or Blood Substitute as its primary modern product definition. We retain those terms because they are historically important, scientifically searchable and still commonly used by clinicians, researchers, journalists and the public.

But the development question is different:

Old ambitionReplace bloodAsk one technology to reproduce a complex biological system.
Modern developmentDefine the functionSpecify oxygen-delivery performance, molecular design, physiological context and measurable endpoints.
BHOC frameworkPrecision Oxygen TherapeuticsEvaluate whether oxygen reaches tissue where and when the physiological need exists.

This framing does not make historical failures disappear. It makes them more useful. They become design requirements: control vascular interaction, characterize oxygen affinity and unloading, protect against oxidation and heme-mediated injury, understand microcirculatory effects, define dose and exposure, and select indications where the benefit–risk question is clinically coherent.

Blood is a system. Oxygen delivery is a function. That is the conceptual bridge from the historical search for a universal blood substitute to the narrower field of Precision Oxygen Therapeutics.
