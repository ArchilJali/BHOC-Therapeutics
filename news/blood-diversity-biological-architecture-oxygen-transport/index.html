Blood Diversity, Biological Architecture and the Future of Oxygen Transport

A Bioengineering Perspective on Transfusion and the BHOC Approach

25 November 2026Archil Jaliashvili

One Oxygen. One Biology. One BHOC System.

1. Blood: One Biological System, Extraordinary Diversity

Blood is among the most extensively studied biological tissues and systems. Its composition, cellular architecture, molecular interactions and physiological functions have been investigated for more than a century. Yet, despite this extensive scientific knowledge, blood remains one of the most complex and biologically diverse systems in nature.

The scale of this complexity becomes even more remarkable when viewed in the context of global biodiversity. Approximately 1.78 million species of animals, plants and microorganisms had been identified and taxonomically described in the historical estimates published by the Convention on Biological Diversity. The actual diversity of life is considerably greater, with millions of species still awaiting discovery and scientific description.

Across the animal kingdom, circulatory systems have evolved into remarkably diverse biological architectures. Their cellular components, oxygen-transport mechanisms, respiratory pigments and molecular adaptations vary considerably. Although some organisms use hemoglobin, others rely on different oxygen-binding molecules or entirely different physiological strategies. Our understanding of these systems remains uneven, and the full diversity of oxygen transport across life is far from completely characterized.

Even within a single species—human beings—the complexity is extraordinary. Human blood contains multiple antigen systems distributed across red blood cells, platelets, leukocytes and plasma-associated components. These systems interact to create an immense range of potential immunological and antigenic configurations. A theoretical combinatorial model of human blood diversity has yielded an estimate of approximately 2.59 × 10134 possible combinations under its defined assumptions. This is not a count of clinically observed blood types, but an illustration of the extraordinary scale of biological diversity.

There is a fundamental principle behind this complexity: a shared biological foundation can generate an extraordinary diversity of structures, interactions and functions.

An analogy can be found in digital information, where just two basic symbols—0 and 1—can encode an immense universe of information through their combinations. Similarly, biological systems use a relatively limited set of fundamental molecular building blocks, genetic instructions and biochemical principles to generate the extraordinary diversity of life.

Hemoglobin is one of these fundamental biological components. Across different evolutionary adaptations, hemoglobin and its variants have served as important molecular platforms for oxygen binding, transport and release. Its significance extends beyond its presence in red blood cells: it represents one of nature's highly developed solutions to a universal physiological requirement—the delivery of oxygen to living tissues.

Modern transfusion medicine has developed sophisticated methods for blood grouping, antibody screening, crossmatching and compatibility assessment. These procedures are essential for transfusion safety. However, compatibility represents only one dimension of blood biology. The physiological performance of blood—particularly oxygen transport, oxygen release and delivery to tissues—is another fundamental dimension that deserves equal scientific attention.

This distinction opens a broader perspective: rather than considering blood exclusively through its cellular composition, antigenic identity or volume, we can also examine it through the biological function it performs.

This is the conceptual foundation of BHOC (Biological Hemoglobin Oxygen Carrier): an approach built around a fundamental biological molecule and its oxygen-transport function, exploring how a defined biological mechanism can be developed into a new bioengineering platform without attempting to reproduce the entire complexity of blood.

The central question is therefore not simply how to reproduce blood, but how to understand, preserve and support one of its most fundamental physiological functions: oxygen transport and delivery to tissues.

2. Theoretical Diversity of Human Blood Antigen Profiles

Human blood contains multiple antigen systems across red blood cells, platelets, leukocytes and plasma. Their combinations create an exceptionally large range of potential antigen profiles.

2.1. Red Blood Cells (RBCs)

  • 49 blood group systems
  • 400 recognized antigens

Assuming two possible states for each antigen:

NRBC = 2400 ≈ 2.58 × 10120

2.2. Platelets (PLTs)

  • 35 HPA systems
  • 41 HPA antigens
  • 6 biallelic systems
  • 29 additional antigenic specificities

For the six biallelic systems, three phenotype states are considered per system. The remaining 29 specificities are represented as binary states:

NPLT = 36 × 229 ≈ 3.91 × 1011

2.3. Leukocytes (WBCs)

  • 5 HNA systems
  • 8 principal antigen specificities
NHNA = 28 = 256

2.4. HLA System

  • HLA class I: A, B, C
  • HLA class II: DR, DQ, DP

HLA contributes a major dimension of immunological diversity across leukocytes, lymphocytes and platelets.

2.5. Lymphocytes

  • T lymphocytes
  • B lymphocytes
  • NK cells
  • Additional immune-cell recognition systems, including KIR

Lymphocytes contribute to the broader immune-recognition architecture of blood. Their HLA expression is considered within the HLA system rather than counted again as a separate antigen category.

2.6. Plasma

Major plasma protein antigen systems include:

  • Gm — IgG
  • Am — IgA
  • Km — immunoglobulin light chains
  • Chido/Rodgers — complement C4

2.7. Combined Numerical Model

Combining the calculated RBC, platelet HPA and leukocyte HNA values:

N = 2400 × 36 × 229 × 28
N ≈ 2.59 × 10134

HLA and plasma profiles are not included in this numerical result. Lymphocytes are part of the leukocyte population and are not counted as an additional independent category.

The platelet calculation uses three phenotype states for each of six biallelic HPA systems and binary states for the remaining 29 antigenic specificities. The combined model assumes independent combinations of the included features.

The result is a number with approximately 135 digits. Even this partial calculation illustrates the extraordinary combinatorial scale of human blood antigen diversity.

3. Beyond Blood Groups: The Functional Dimension

Blood compatibility and blood function are related, but they are not identical concepts.

Conventional transfusion assessment focuses on parameters such as:

  • ABO and Rh blood groups;
  • red-cell antigen compatibility;
  • hemoglobin concentration;
  • hematocrit;
  • circulating blood volume;
  • antibody screening and crossmatching.

These measurements are indispensable. However, the biological performance of oxygen transport also depends on other factors:

  • hemoglobin concentration and molecular properties;
  • oxygen affinity and dissociation;
  • oxygen partial pressure;
  • microvascular distribution;
  • diffusion across the capillary-tissue interface;
  • mitochondrial oxygen utilization.

The presence of hemoglobin does not, by itself, describe the complete process of oxygen delivery. Oxygen must be transported, released at the appropriate physiological location and made available for cellular metabolism.

This creates an opportunity to examine transfusion and oxygen support through a broader bioengineering perspective.

4. Hemoglobin: An Evolutionary Architecture of Oxygen Transport

Hemoglobin is one of the most important biological molecules in the history of oxygen-dependent life. Its structure and function have diversified across species through evolutionary adaptation to different physiological environments. Differences in oxygen affinity, molecular organization and regulation reflect the requirements of organisms living under very different conditions.

An interesting structural relationship also exists between hemoglobin and chlorophyll. Both contain related tetrapyrrole-based molecular architectures. However, their central metals and biological functions differ:

  • Hemoglobin: iron-containing heme, specialized for oxygen binding and transport.
  • Chlorophyll: magnesium-containing porphyrin structure, specialized for light absorption and photosynthesis.

They are not functionally equivalent molecules. Their related structural frameworks nevertheless illustrate how nature uses related molecular architectures in different biological systems.

The evolution of hemoglobin demonstrates how a conserved molecular foundation can be adapted into highly specialized oxygen-transport systems.

5. From Molecular Properties to Tissue Oxygenation

Oxygen transport is not simply a matter of carrying oxygen within the circulation. It is a coordinated physiological process involving several levels:

  1. Molecular oxygen binding.
  2. Oxygen loading and transport.
  3. Oxygen release under local physiological conditions.
  4. Distribution through the microcirculation.
  5. Diffusion into surrounding tissues.
  6. Cellular and mitochondrial utilization.

A functional approach to oxygen therapeutics must consider this entire pathway. The central question is not only how much oxygen is carried, but also how effectively oxygen can be delivered where it is needed and made available for cellular metabolism.

This perspective connects molecular biophysics with vascular physiology, tissue metabolism and organ function.

6. BHOC: Building on the Biological Architecture of Hemoglobin

BHOC — Biological Hemoglobin Oxygen Carrier — is being developed within the broader field of Precision Oxygen Therapeutics.

The concept builds on the biological architecture of hemoglobin-mediated oxygen transport. BHOC is designed to support oxygen transport when native oxygen-carrying capacity is insufficient. Its purpose is not to replace red blood cells as a whole, but to provide an additional means of supporting oxygen transport and tissue oxygen delivery.

The approach focuses on the functional properties of hemoglobin and their relevance to physiological oxygenation.

This creates a broader framework for research into:

  • oxygen transport and release;
  • microvascular oxygen delivery;
  • tissue oxygenation;
  • mitochondrial oxygen availability;
  • organ preservation and sustainability;
  • emergency and critical-care applications;
  • veterinary and human therapeutic development.

The BHOC concept therefore belongs to a developing scientific direction that connects molecular engineering, biophysics and clinical physiology.

7. A New Perspective: Precision Oxygen Therapeutics

The extraordinary diversity of blood antigen profiles illustrates the complexity of biological compatibility. At the same time, the fundamental physiological role of blood remains closely connected to oxygen transport and tissue metabolism.

These two dimensions should be considered together:

  • Biological compatibility concerns the interaction between blood components and the immune system.
  • Functional oxygenation concerns the capacity to transport, release and deliver oxygen to tissues.

A bioengineering approach can bring these dimensions into a broader framework, expanding scientific investigation beyond conventional blood classification and quantity-based assessment.

Precision Oxygen Therapeutics aims to advance this direction through the integration of molecular biophysics, oxygen transport, microcirculation, tissue metabolism and organ sustainability. The objective is to understand and support oxygen delivery with greater physiological precision.

Conclusion

Blood is a highly complex immunological organ; transfusion should be understood as a form of transplantation.

Its antigenic diversity demonstrates the extraordinary complexity of biological individuality. Its hemoglobin architecture demonstrates the evolutionary sophistication of oxygen transport.

The next stage of development should connect these perspectives: from molecular structure to physiological function, from oxygen transport to tissue metabolism, and from conventional transfusion concepts to precision oxygen therapeutics.

BHOC builds on this biological foundation.
