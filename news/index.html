Two advanced blood systems, one recurring signal: donor blood remains a logistics-dependent resource

Blood shortages are often discussed as a problem of low-resource health systems. The latest signals from Scotland and Australia show that supply resilience can become fragile even inside highly organized national blood services.

On 7 September 2026, the Scottish National Blood Transfusion Service reported only three days of O-positive red-cell stock, around half of its normal operational target of approximately six days. In Australia, the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood classified O-positive, O-negative and A-positive red-cell stocks as Low, meaning additional donors were needed immediately to stabilize supply.

These are not failures of transfusion medicine. Donor blood remains essential and life-saving. They are reminders of a structural reality: blood must be donated, typed, tested, processed, stored, transported and available in the correct group at the place and moment it is needed.

That constraint becomes especially important in trauma, prehospital emergency care, postpartum hemorrhage, remote medicine and mass-casualty settings. The clinical question is not donor blood versus another technology. It is how to maintain effective tissue oxygen delivery during the interval before compatible blood is available.

BHOC perspective: Precision Oxygen Therapeutics should be developed as a complementary bridge capability for situations where donor blood is unavailable, delayed or logistically constrained. Emergency oxygen delivery should be more resilient than any single supply chain.
