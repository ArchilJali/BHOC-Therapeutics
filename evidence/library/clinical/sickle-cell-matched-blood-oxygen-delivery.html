SCA / SCD · Precision Oxygen Therapeutics

Sickle cell disease.
Oxygen has to reach the tissue.

Sickle cell disease (SCD), including sickle cell anemia (SCA), affects red cells and their oxygen-delivery function. Hemoglobin S (HbS), blood flow and tissue oxygenation need to be understood together.

Reading guide updated 8 November 2026. Clinical analysis and sources below.

01 · THE PROBLEM

Sickle cells and blood flow

Sickling describes red cells taking on a sickle-like shape. Red-cell breakdown and impaired flow through small vessels can disrupt oxygen delivery. Hemoglobin concentration is only one part of this picture.

02 · TRANSFUSION SUPPORT

Add cells, or exchange cells

Simple transfusion adds compatible donor red cells. Red-cell exchange also removes the patient's red cells and reduces the proportion of HbS.

03 · BHOC RESEARCH

Support oxygen delivery

Can a cell-free hemoglobin carrier add tissue-oxygenation support alongside care that addresses sickling and its complications? This is the BHOC research question.

Total Hb, HbS and tissue oxygenation answer different questions.

Total Hb: how much hemoglobin?

Total hemoglobin includes HbS. A higher total Hb does not, by itself, show that oxygen delivery to tissues has recovered.

HbS fraction: how much is HbS?

The proportion of hemoglobin S is a separate measurement. Lowering HbS and increasing total Hb are different treatment aims.

Tissue oxygenation: does oxygen arrive?

Blood flow and oxygen loading and unloading matter alongside hemoglobin concentration. Tissue oxygen delivery needs its own assessment.

The BHOC research question

Can a cell-free oxygen carrier provide additional tissue support?

BHOC, a Biological Hemoglobin Oxygen Carrier, is designed to carry oxygen in plasma, outside red cells. The development aim is controlled, sustained oxygen delivery adapted to tissue demand. In SCA/SCD, the question is whether a cell-free carrier can support tissue oxygenation alongside established disease-specific care.

This research question extends beyond a delay in obtaining donor blood. Studies need to distinguish effects on tissue oxygenation and microvascular flow from HbS reduction, and evaluate safety and clinical outcomes.

Investigational research direction. A clinical benefit in SCA/SCD requires product-specific evidence. The historical HBOC studies below provide context for the question.

Sickle Cell Disease: Matched Blood and Oxygen Delivery

Matched Blood Remains Essential

What can medicine do when compatible red cells cannot be obtained in time, or when transfusion does not restore oxygen delivery as expected?

Evidence analysis · Archil Jaliashvili · 8 October 2026

The current access gap

NHS Blood and Transplant reports that the NHS can currently meet only around half of the blood demand for patients with sickle cell who rely on regular transfusions. More than 19,000 people are living with sickle cell disorder in England, and NHSBT estimates that at least 16,000 additional Black-heritage donors are needed.

This is not simply a shortage of total blood units. Many patients need red cells matched beyond ABO and RhD. Donors of Black heritage are more likely to carry the Ro subtype needed by many patients with sickle cell disorder.

Simple transfusion and red-cell exchange are not the same intervention

Simple transfusion

Adds donor red cells. It can raise haemoglobin and oxygen-carrying capacity, but also increases donor-cell exposure and may add iron over repeated treatment.

Red-cell exchange

Removes sickled red cells while replacing them with donor cells. It can reduce the proportion of haemoglobin S without the same net iron loading as repeated simple transfusion, but requires compatible units and specialist infrastructure.

One patient described by NHSBT receives exchange every seven weeks using seven to eight donated units per procedure. This illustrates both the value of the treatment and its continuing dependence on matched donor supply.

Alloimmunization and haemolytic complications

Exposure to donor red-cell antigens can lead a patient to form antibodies. This alloimmunization may make future matching more difficult. NHSBT has reported that approximately 17% of adults with sickle cell disorder are alloimmunized.

Delayed haemolytic transfusion reactions and hyperhaemolysis are particularly serious complications. In hyperhaemolysis, both transfused and the patient's own red cells may be destroyed, and haemoglobin can fall below the pre-transfusion level.

Mewha and colleagues documented continued exposure to donor antigens in chronically transfused patients with SCD despite Rh and Kell matching and RH genotyping. Read the 2026 study brief on red cell transfusion compatibility.

Clinical boundary: transfusion can be lifesaving and matched red-cell exchange remains essential. This analysis does not recommend withholding indicated transfusion. It identifies situations in which further donor-cell exposure requires specialist risk assessment.

Why haemoglobin concentration is not the whole answer

A rise in haemoglobin does not by itself prove that tissue oxygen delivery has been restored. Effective delivery also depends on circulation, microvascular flow, oxygen loading and unloading, red-cell deformability, vascular regulation and the patient's metabolic demand.

When haemolysis, vaso-occlusion, inflammation or microvascular dysfunction continues, another transfusion may not correct every cause of impaired oxygen delivery. In selected immune-mediated complications it may also expose the patient to additional donor-cell antigens and additional cells that can become targets of haemolysis.

When compatible red cells are delayed, or when transfusion cannot safely or efficiently restore oxygen delivery, how can oxygen-delivery function be supported while the immune process is treated and definitive care is prepared?

This is a research question, not a claim that any currently unproven product can replace matched donor blood.

Sickle cell anemia, HbS and oxygen-delivery endpoints

The matched-blood analysis above separates the donor-blood system from the oxygen-delivery question. A study in sickle cell disease should identify whether it is measuring HbS reduction, compatibility, oxygen transport, microvascular response or a clinical outcome.

The historical catalogue includes a phase I/II polymerized-bovine-hemoglobin study in adults with sickle cell disease outside crisis, and a separate three-case HBOC-201 report during sickle crisis. These are different populations and study designs. They do not establish controlled BHOC efficacy in acute sickle crisis.
